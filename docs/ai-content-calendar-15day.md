**[SLIDE 2 — TERM 1]**
Term: **LLM (Large Language Model)**
Plain English: The engine that powers ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini.
Think of it as: A brain that learned from the entire internet — billions of books, articles, and conversations — and can now respond to your questions in human language.
What it cannot do: Look things up in real time (unless given specific tools to do so).
Why you need to know this: Every AI writing, research, or analysis tool you'll encounter is built on an LLM.

---

**[SLIDE 3 — TERM 2]**
Term: **Prompt**
Plain English: What you type into an AI to get a response.
The secret most people miss: The quality of what AI gives you is 100% determined by the quality of what you ask it. A vague prompt gets a vague answer. A specific, well-structured prompt gets a specific, useful answer.
Why you need to know this: Prompt engineering — the skill of writing great prompts — is the #1 most valuable AI skill you can develop right now. No coding required.

---

**[SLIDE 4 — TERM 3]**
Term: **Context Window**
Plain English: How much of a conversation an AI can "remember" at once.
Think of it as: The AI's working memory. If you're having a very long conversation, early details may fall outside the AI's memory window.
Practical impact: For long projects, re-give the AI key context at the start of each session. Treat it like briefing a new person each time.
Why you need to know this: Knowing this helps you troubleshoot when AI seems to "forget" what you told it.

---

**[SLIDE 5 — TERM 4]**
Term: **Hallucination**
Plain English: When AI confidently states something that is completely made up.
Think of it as: A brilliant intern who will guess confidently rather than admit they don't know.
Real example: AI may generate a fake court case citation, a statistic with a wrong source, or a quote that was never said.
Why you need to know this: Always verify AI-generated facts, statistics, dates, and citations before using them in professional documents or client work.

---

**[SLIDE 6 — TERM 5]**
Term: **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)**
Plain English: Giving AI access to your specific documents so it answers based on YOUR data — not generic internet information.
Think of it as: Training AI on your company's internal knowledge — your SOPs, client files, past proposals, meeting notes.
Business use case: "Here are our last 10 client contracts. Draft a new one for this specific client."
Why you need to know this: This is how enterprise teams are making AI genuinely useful for proprietary, confidential information.

---

**[SLIDE 7 — TERM 6]**
Term: **AI Agent**
Plain English: An AI that can take actions on your behalf — not just answer questions.
Think of it as: An AI assistant that can browse the web, write and send emails, update your CRM, book calendar slots, and execute multi-step tasks automatically.
Where it is right now: Early-stage but rapidly maturing. 2025–2026 is the year agents go mainstream for business users.
Why you need to know this: Agents represent the next wave of AI productivity. Understanding what they can do puts you ahead of 95% of professionals.

The gap between those two results is called prompt engineering — and it's a learnable skill that takes less than an hour to develop.

---

**[SLIDE 3 — THE ANATOMY: PART 1]**
Headline: "A great prompt has 5 parts."
Part 1: **ROLE**
What it means: Tell the AI who to be before you ask it anything.
Bad: "Write me a summary of this meeting."
Better: "You are a senior executive assistant with 15 years of experience supporting C-suite leaders. Write a summary of this meeting."

Why it works: AI performs significantly better when given a specific persona. It shifts the tone, vocabulary, and depth of the response to match the expert you described.

---

**[SLIDE 4 — THE ANATOMY: PART 2]**
Part 2: **CONTEXT**
What it means: Give the AI the background it needs to be genuinely useful.
Bad: "Write a proposal."
Better: "I am a marketing consultant pitching a 3-month brand strategy project to a mid-size e-commerce company that sells sustainable outdoor gear. Their main challenge is that their conversion rate is under 1%. They have a $15K budget."

Why it works: The more specific context you provide, the more tailored and accurate the output. AI has no knowledge of your situation unless you share it.

---

**[SLIDE 5 — THE ANATOMY: PART 3]**
Part 3: **TASK**
What it means: State exactly what you want done — and in what format.
Bad: "Write something about this."
Better: "Write a 300-word executive summary with 3 key sections: Problem, Proposed Solution, and Expected Outcomes. Use professional language suitable for a board-level audience."

Why it works: Specifying length, structure, format, and audience gives AI a precise target to hit instead of guessing what "good" looks like.

---

**[SLIDE 6 — THE ANATOMY: PART 4]**
Part 4: **TONE & STYLE**
What it means: Tell the AI how it should sound.
Bad: (no tone instruction)
Better: "Write this in a confident, direct tone. Use short sentences. Avoid jargon. This should read like it was written by a trusted advisor, not a textbook."

Why it works: Without tone guidance, AI defaults to a generic, slightly corporate voice. With tone guidance, the output sounds like a real person — ideally, like you.

---

**[SLIDE 7 — THE ANATOMY: PART 5]**
Part 5: **CONSTRAINTS**
What it means: Tell the AI what NOT to do.
Examples:
→ "Do not include generic platitudes or filler phrases."
→ "Do not recommend any tool that costs more than $50/month."
→ "Do not use passive voice."
→ "Do not repeat information from the previous section."

Why it works: Constraints are where most prompts fail. Telling AI what to avoid is just as important as telling it what to produce.

---

**[SLIDE 8 — THE FULL FORMULA]**
Headline: "The full prompt formula:"
Format (text laid out clearly):

ROLE: You are [specific expert/persona].
CONTEXT: [Background information, situation, relevant details].
TASK: [Exactly what you want produced, in what format, at what length].
TONE: [How it should sound / who it's for].
CONSTRAINTS: [What to avoid, what rules to follow].

Here's how it works:

---

**C — CHARACTER**
Define who the AI should be before asking it anything.

Not: "Help me with this."
But: "You are a senior mergers and acquisitions attorney with 20 years of experience advising mid-market companies. You have a reputation for translating complex legal concepts into plain language that executives can act on."

The character instruction fundamentally changes how AI frames its response — the vocabulary it uses, the assumptions it makes, the depth it goes to.

Spend 2 sentences on character. It is the highest-leverage part of the prompt.

---

**R — REQUEST**
State exactly what you want. Be surgical.

Include:
→ The specific deliverable (not "help me" — "write a 250-word executive summary")
→ The format (bullet list, numbered steps, narrative prose, table)
→ The length (word count or section count)
→ The audience (who will read this, and what do they need to walk away knowing)

Vague requests produce vague responses. The more precisely you define the output, the more precisely AI can target it.

---

**A — ADDED CONTEXT**
Give the AI everything it needs that it cannot know on its own.

This is where most prompts fail — professionals assume AI knows their situation. It doesn't.

Add:
→ Background on the person, company, or project
→ Relevant history or prior decisions
→ Constraints unique to your situation
→ The emotional or political context (stakeholder concerns, sensitivities, power dynamics)

The more context you provide, the less generic the output becomes.

---

**F — FORMAT & TONE**
Specify exactly how the output should look and sound.

Format: "Use headers. Limit each section to 3 bullet points. Lead with the most important finding."

Tone: "Confident but not arrogant. Direct but not cold. This will be read by a skeptical CFO — speak to ROI, not possibility."

Without explicit format and tone instructions, AI defaults to a generic, mid-level corporate voice. With them, the output sounds like it was written by a specific expert for a specific audience.

---

**T — TEST FOR GAPS**
Before submitting, read your prompt back to yourself and ask:

→ If I gave this to a brilliant new consultant on their first day, would they know exactly what to produce?
→ Have I told them who they are, what I need, and how it should sound?
→ What is the single most important thing this output needs to accomplish — and have I made that explicit?

If any of those answers are "no" — add one more line to your prompt.

The extra 60 seconds on the prompt saves 20 minutes of editing the output.

*"You are [SPECIFIC EXPERT / CHARACTER]. I need you to [EXACT DELIVERABLE, FORMAT, LENGTH, AUDIENCE]. Here is the relevant context: [BACKGROUND, SITUATION, HISTORY, CONSTRAINTS]. # CONTENT CREATION NOTES

**For carousel posts:** Design each slide at 1080x1080px (square) or 1080x1350px (portrait — gets more real estate in feed). Use a consistent brand template — same fonts, same colors, same layout grid across all 15 days. Consistency builds brand recognition faster than variety.

**For LinkedIn posts:** Do not add a link in the post body — LinkedIn's algorithm suppresses reach on posts with external links. Put any links in the first comment. Use line breaks aggressively — every 2–3 sentences gets its own line. This dramatically improves readability on mobile and increases time-on-post.

**For all posts:** The first line is everything. On LinkedIn, readers see 2–3 lines before "see more." On Instagram, readers see the first line of the caption before "more." Write every first line to earn the click.

**Hashtag strategy for Instagram:** Use 5–15 hashtags. Mix: 3 large (#AITools — 1M+ posts), 5 medium (#AIForBusiness — 100K–1M posts), 5 niche (#AIConsultingFirm — under 100K posts). Do not use the same hashtag set on every post — vary them by topic.

**Engagement window:** For the first 30–60 minutes after posting, respond to every comment. The algorithm reads your response rate as a signal of content quality. A post with 10 comments you responded to outperforms a post with 10 ignored comments.

**Cross-posting rule:** Adapt, do not copy. LinkedIn posts are too long for Instagram captions. Instagram captions need hashtags that LinkedIn does not. Day 15 shows you the adapted version — use that model for any cross-platform content.
